The problem

Two things that have always been true.

Construction recruitment runs almost entirely on WhatsApp, instinct, and a commission structure that most consultants navigate by feel. There's no shortage of hard work. There is a shortage of clarity.

Problem one
It burns people out

Not in a vague way — in a structural way. Open loops accumulate. Priorities compete. The job creates more unresolved cognitive load than most people can sustainably carry, and the good ones leave because of it.

Problem two
It rewards the wrong things short-term

The consultants who last are the ones who play the long game — relationships, quality placements, retained workers. But the environment pulls hard toward speed and short-termism. It takes unusual discipline to resist that.

They're connected. The consultant who's always reactive and always depleted is more likely to make short-term decisions that damage the relationships that would have compounded into real income.

Fix the first and you start to fix the second. That's the idea, anyway.

Every one of those open loops persists in the background whether the consultant is actively thinking about it or not. That's not a motivation problem or a time management problem. It's just how attention works.

The commission picture

Most consultants navigate this entirely by feel.

The standard commission structure in construction recruitment is non-linear. Cross certain monthly earnings thresholds and the rate you earn jumps. Below £5k in a month, nothing pays out at all. Above £20k, every additional £1,000 earned pays the consultant £200. Most consultants don't know which tier they're in on any given day.

Monthly earnings are a function of the margin between what a client gets billed and what the worker gets paid, multiplied across hours worked and number of workers placed.

charge rate − pay rate × hours worked × number of workers = monthly earnings

The compounding argument is simple when you see it written down. A consultant who builds a retained base of workers billing every week starts each month with a floor — already partway up the tier structure before making a single new placement. A consultant who churns through placements starts from zero every month.

Starting from zero every month
Must clear £5k floor before anything pays out
High effort, high variance, income resets monthly
Every placement earns once, then disappears
Ceiling determined by how much you can physically do
Starting with a retained base
Floor earnings before the month has started
New placements immediately earn at a higher rate
Placement effort pays out for weeks, not days
Income becomes more predictable, less effort-dependent

This isn't a secret. Most experienced consultants know it instinctively. The question is why so few operate this way — and whether the environment can be designed to make it easier.

The thinking

Three things worth optimising at the same time.

The way we see it, a consultant's working life sits across three dimensions. Most tools and systems — if they exist at all — address one. We think the interesting problem is all three, simultaneously, and the way they interact.

Foundation
Wellbeing
  • Cognitive load — open loops held in working memory
  • Emotional load — the weight of competing priorities
  • Physical load — pace and volume over time
  • Meaning — whether the work feels like it's going somewhere
Performance
Commission
  • Tier position and month-to-date earnings
  • Retained base and monthly floor value
  • Placement timing within the month
  • Year-end trajectory, not just this month
Quality
Service
  • Placing people well, not just fast
  • Client relationships that compound over time
  • Candidate experience that generates referrals
  • Long-term retention, not short-term fills

Wellbeing sits underneath the other two because it sets the ceiling on both. A consultant who's depleted makes worse placement decisions and delivers worse service — regardless of how good they are at the job on a good day. That's not a character observation, it's just how attention works under load.

What attention actually costs

Open loops are expensive. Not in the time they take — in the attention they hold. A message left unresolved doesn't go away when you close the app. It keeps running in the background, competing with whatever you're supposed to be doing next. The load comes in four forms.

Cognitive
Open loops and decisions held in working memory without resolution
Emotional
The weight of competing priorities and unresolved situations
Physical
Fatigue from sustained volume and constant context-switching
Meaning
Whether the work feels connected to something worth doing

Some things that seem obvious when you say them out loud

These are the hypotheses we're working from. We think they're right. We're building partly to find out.

01
The consultant who builds well earns more than the one who grinds.
Over any meaningful time horizon, a retained base of billing workers beats a constant stream of new placements — financially and in terms of the energy required to sustain it. Most consultants know this. The environment makes it hard to act on.
02
If someone's head is full of open loops, they can't think straight.
That's not a theory, it's just Tuesday. Reducing that load — not managing it but genuinely reducing it — changes what someone is capable of. A system that closes loops rather than just surfacing them is doing something meaningfully different.
03
Momentum is the thing that keeps people going. Stalling is what burns them out.
A lot of burnout in this industry isn't from working too hard. It's from working hard and not being able to see whether it's working. Make the progress visible and the energy tends to follow.
04
One clear priority beats a list of ten every time.
Not a dashboard, not a task list — the single thing that matters most right now given everything else that's going on. That's a harder problem than it looks. But it's the right problem to solve.
What we're building

The idea, in plain English.

Construction recruitment runs on WhatsApp. That's not a problem — it's just how the industry works, and it works because it's fast and personal. But all of that context lives in a phone and exists nowhere else. The consultant holds everything in their head. Nobody can see the full picture.

The tool we're building reads that existing WhatsApp context, understands the relationships and situations inside it, and gives the consultant one clear thing to focus on at any given moment — accounting for where they are in the month, what's active, and what they're trying to achieve. An AI assistant handles the routine alongside them. The stuff that needs a human gets a human.

No behaviour change required. They already use WhatsApp. They've already built all this context. It just needs to be readable and useful rather than overwhelming.

4
AI copilot
Handles the routine alongside the consultant — follow-ups, admin, check-ins. The stuff that doesn't need their full attention gets handled without them having to think about it.
3
One priority at a time
The single most important action right now, updated continuously as new messages arrive. Not a list. One thing — the thing that actually moves something forward given the full picture.
2
Situational awareness
A clear picture of where things actually stand — active placements, commission position, what's open, what needs attention. What is true right now, not what to do about it.
1
Relationship intelligence
Everything from the consultant's WhatsApp history — every client, candidate, worker, and situation — read, understood, and made useful. The existing context, made visible for the first time.

The goal is simple even if the build isn't: fewer open loops, clearer sense of what matters, less of the day spent reacting and more spent doing the thing that actually moves something forward.
